Why mental institutions can harm people

Opinion, with sources

Most of the harm in psychiatric institutions comes from how they are built, not from a wish to hurt anyone. Any place that holds people for a long time, under someone else's control, carries the same risks.

Loss of control

The sociologist Erving Goffman called asylums "total institutions." In them, staff schedule and watch every part of daily life. Long stays can wear down the everyday skills and confidence people need once they leave.

Coercion and trauma

Being held against your will, physically restrained, or locked alone in a room can be terrifying. Some patients come out more afraid of care than before. That fear can stop them from asking for help the next time they need it.

A history of abuse

Overcrowded state asylums and places like Willowbrook State School in New York became known for neglect and abuse. Reporting in the 1970s showed conditions there to the public. That history still shapes how much people trust these places.

Not being believed

Once someone has a diagnosis, staff can read ordinary behavior as a symptom. The famous Rosenhan "pseudopatient" study made this point in 1973. Later research by the journalist Susannah Cahalan raised serious doubts about whether the study was carried out as reported, so treat it with caution.

Isolation and stigma

Being taken away from family, work, and community can make recovery harder. A hospital stay can also follow a person afterward, in how others see them and in how they see themselves.

Counterpoint: When the large asylums closed, the community care meant to replace them was often never funded. Many people with serious mental illness ended up homeless or in jail, and some US jails now hold more people with mental illness than any hospital. In an acute crisis, a short stay in a modern psychiatric unit can save a life. Most research supports care in the community, with hospital stays kept short and used when needed.

What this does not say

This page does not say you should avoid the hospital if you need it. A short stay can be the safest option during a crisis. The goal is care that keeps your dignity, your rights, and your ties to the people in your life.

If you or someone close to you may need hospital care:
  • Ask whether a voluntary admission is possible. It usually gives you more say in your care.
  • Ask for a copy of your rights as a patient. Hospitals are generally required to provide one.
  • Ask about crisis stabilization units, day programs, or mobile crisis teams, which can be alternatives to a long stay.
  • Bring a trusted person into the conversation if you can.

Sources and further reading:

  • Goffman, E. (1961). Asylums: Essays on the Social Situation of Mental Patients and Other Inmates.
  • Rosenhan, D. L. (1973). "On Being Sane in Insane Places." Science.
  • Cahalan, S. (2019). The Great Pretender, which questions the Rosenhan study.
  • nami.org, the National Alliance on Mental Illness, for patient and family guidance.
  • samhsa.gov, the US Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, for crisis care and community services.
