A personal page from Planet Hoto

I hate Scientology.
Here's how to stay away from it.

This is my opinion, and I hold it firmly. Scientology is a pay-to-progress organization that sells “auditing” and courses in escalating tiers, pressures members to cut off family who criticize it, and has a long, well-documented record of going after former members and journalists. I want nothing to do with it, and I don't want you to get pulled in either.

Not a church I respect

The reasons below are not secrets. They come from court records, investigative books and documentaries, and the accounts of hundreds of former members, including people who spent decades inside at senior levels.

  • It costs a fortune. Advancing up “The Bridge” means paying for course after course. Ex-members routinely describe spending six figures over the years.
  • Disconnection. Members are pushed to cut contact with relatives and friends labeled “suppressive.” Families get split for years.
  • Fair Game. Hubbard's own policy declared that enemies may be “tricked, sued or lied to or destroyed.” The organization says it was cancelled. Critics and ex-members say the behavior never stopped.
  • The Sea Org. Its inner workforce signs a billion-year contract, works punishing hours for a tiny stipend, and has described forced labor and confinement in detail.
  • Secrecy by price tag. The core beliefs about Xenu and body thetans are withheld until you've paid your way to OT III. A faith that hides its own doctrine behind a paywall is selling something.
  • It attacks the care this site exists to explain. Through its front group CCHR, Scientology campaigns against psychiatry and psychiatric medication, and tells people in crisis that the help they need is a fraud.

Recognize the pitch

How they approach you

Recruitment almost never starts with the word “Scientology.” It starts with something free and vague. If you learn these openers, you'll spot them from across the street.

“Free personality test”

The Oxford Capacity Analysis. It has nothing to do with Oxford. Two hundred questions, then a “results session” that tells you you're depressed, anxious or unstable and that they have the fix. Everyone's results are bad. That's the point.

“Free stress test”

A folding table on a sidewalk or at a fair, with a small device with two metal cans attached. That's an E-meter. You hold the cans, the needle moves, and you're told your stress is “located.” The remedy is a Dianetics book and a visit to the org.

Dianetics book tables

The volcano cover. Sold cheap or handed out, often with a pitch about “the mind's hidden potential.” The book is the funnel into the paid courses.

“Communication course” or “free film”

An invite to a short course on confidence, study skills, relationships, or a free screening about “the mind.” The course is real, the follow-up is a sales interview. Registrars are trained closers.

Online quizzes and ads

Sponsored ads for “Are you happy?” tests, Dianetics, or “how to boost your IQ.” They harvest your phone number and email, then call. And call.

Through a friend or a workplace

Members are expected to recruit. A coworker's “business seminar,” a tutoring program, a drug-education talk, or a disaster-relief volunteer group can all be routes in. The next section lists the names to watch for.

Know the names

Front groups

These organizations are run by Scientologists, promote L. Ron Hubbard's methods, and in many cases are formally licensed by Scientology entities. Some don't mention the connection anywhere on their websites. If one of them shows up at your school, clinic, workplace or town council, that's your cue.

In the moment

How to refuse

You never owe a recruiter a conversation. The pitch depends on politeness, curiosity and the awkwardness of walking away. Remove all three.

  • Don't take the test. Not “just for fun.” The test exists to open a sales conversation about your flaws.
  • Don't give your name, number or email. No sign-in sheet, no raffle, no “we'll send you the results.” Once they have a number, calls can go on for years.
  • Don't go inside the building. A “quick tour” or “free film” puts you in a room with a trained closer and no easy exit.
  • Don't sign anything. Course enrollment forms contain releases and arbitration clauses that have been used against former members.
  • Don't debate. Arguing about Xenu or Hubbard's biography keeps you at the table. Leaving ends it.
  • Pay nothing. Not for a book, not for a “donation,” not for the sauna. The first payment is the hook.
What to say “No thanks, I'm not interested.” Then keep walking. You don't have to explain, soften it, or wait for a reply.
If they already have your number Block it. Don't ask them to stop calling, don't explain why, don't answer to confirm it's you. Each answered call gets logged as interest.

Family and friends

If someone you know is in

The most important thing you can do for a member is stay in contact without a fight. Disconnection is Scientology's lever. Give them no reason to pull it.

  • Don't attack the group head-on. Members are trained to treat criticism as proof you're “suppressive.” Arguing hands the org a reason to cut you off.
  • Keep the relationship warm. Birthdays, dinners, ordinary life. People leave when they have somewhere to land.
  • Ask questions instead of making statements. “How much has it cost so far?” and “What happens if you want to stop?” do more than any argument.
  • Don't send money. Even a loan for rent can end up as a course payment.
  • Know the exit resources. People who leave, especially from the Sea Org, often have no money, no ID, no work history and no outside friends.

Pocket version

Quick checklist

  • ✓Free “personality” or “stress” test on a table? Keep walking.
  • ✓A book with a volcano on it? That's Dianetics. Decline it.
  • ✓Unfamiliar education, rehab or “human rights” group? Search its name with “Hubbard.”
  • ✓Never give your phone number or email. Never sign. Never pay.
  • ✓Already being called? Block, don't argue.
  • ✓Someone you love is in? Stay close, stay calm, keep the door open.

This page reflects my own views. The practices described are drawn from public court filings, investigative journalism and the published accounts of former members. The Church of Scientology disputes many of those accounts.
