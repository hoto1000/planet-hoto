Community organizations: find one, join one, link them up

Most help does not come from a hospital. It comes from a room with folding chairs, a group chat, a church basement, a library table. This page is about those rooms: how to find one near you, what to say when you walk in, and a plan for small groups in the same area to work together instead of alone.

What kind of page this is: the first half is a sourced explainer. The second half, under "The push," is a proposal. It is our view, labeled as our view.

What counts as a community organization

Any group that people run for the people around them. It does not need an office, a budget, or a logo. Some examples:

  • Peer support groups. People who have been through it, meeting to talk. Many are free and need no sign-up.
  • Mutual aid groups. Neighbors trading food, rides, childcare, or a little money, with no application.
  • Clubhouses. Daytime places run together with members living with mental illness. Work, meals, and company.
  • Warmlines. Phone lines run by peers, for support before anything becomes a crisis.
  • Faith communities and cultural associations. Often the first place people in a neighborhood actually go.
  • Tenant unions, worker centers, neighborhood associations. Help with the problems that cause the stress.
  • Food pantries, community fridges, free clinics.
  • Public libraries. A reference librarian can find almost anything, and will not ask why.
  • Gardens, sports leagues, youth programs, maker spaces. Belonging counts too.

How to find one near you

  1. Call 211 (United States). One number for food, housing, bills, health, mental health, and substance use. 211 describes its help as confidential and available 24/7. You can also search by address or ZIP code on 211.org.
  2. Search findhelp.org. Enter a ZIP code and a word like "food" or "counseling," then narrow by what fits you. findhelp.org
  3. Find your local NAMI. Local NAMI groups run free peer support groups and classes. The NAMI HelpLine is 800-950-6264, Monday to Friday, 10 a.m. to 10 p.m. ET, or text "NAMI" to 62640. Find your local NAMI
  4. Try a warmline. NAMI keeps a directory of peer-run warmlines by state, with hours and whether they take calls from other states. A warmline is for support, not emergencies. In a crisis, use 988 or 911. Warmline directory
  5. Look for a Clubhouse. Clubhouse International lists more than 380 Clubhouses in 30 countries, by city and country. Clubhouse directory
  6. Ask a librarian. Walk up to the reference desk and say what you are looking for. Libraries keep local lists that never make it online.
  7. Ask the people who already help you. A nurse, a teacher, a pastor, a barber, a coach. They know who is good.

What to say when you call or walk in

"Hi. I'm looking for a group or a program that helps with [housing / food / someone to talk to]. I'm in [your town]. Is that something you do?"

"If not, who should I ask?"

That last question is the whole trick. Every "no" should come with a name. If it does not, ask again.

What to expect at a first meeting

You can sit and listen. You do not have to share. You can leave early. Most peer groups will not ask for a diagnosis, an ID, or money.

If a group pressures you for money, personal information, or loyalty, or makes leaving feel like a betrayal, that is a reason to go. We wrote a whole page about one group that does this.

Why small groups matter

Small organizations do most of the work and get the least credit. A pantry, a Tuesday night peer group, a tenant union, a cultural club. Each one knows its people.

Few of them know each other. So someone asks one group for help, hears "we don't do that," and gives up. That gap is where people get lost.

The push: link small organizations together

Shelf: Ideas for change. This is a proposal and our opinion.

Our point
Small groups in the same area should act like one network while staying themselves. Not a merger. Not a committee. A shared list, a monthly hour, and the habit of handing people to each other by name.
Why this matters to us
Because the person who gets bounced three times does not try a fourth. And because a small group working alone burns out, while the same group with nine friends lasts.
What it looks like
  1. One shared list. Every group in the area on one page: what you do, who you serve, when you are open, who to call. Put it on 211 and findhelp too. Most local 211s have an "add your agency" form, and organizations can claim their program on findhelp.org.
  2. One hour a month. A standing call or a coffee, same time every month, for whoever shows up. No minutes required. Once people have met, handoffs turn into text messages between people who know each other.
  3. Warm handoffs. "Let me text Maria and tell her you're coming" beats a phone number on a flyer. Agree on two things: who to call at each group, and that nobody says "we don't do that" without a name.
  4. Share what is expensive. A room, insurance, a bookkeeper, training. A group without nonprofit status can use fiscal sponsorship: an existing nonprofit holds the money and the paperwork, usually for a fee, so donors can give and funders can fund.
  5. One joint event a year. A resource fair in a school gym or a park. Everyone brings a table. A person meets ten groups in an hour.
Who could be affected, and what might we be missing
Small groups can fear losing their identity, or competing for the same grant. Volunteer time is already thin. Some groups should stay separate for safety, like groups for survivors. And a network that turns into a committee helps nobody.
What would make us rethink it
If linking up made help slower, less safe, or less private for the people who use it.
One step we want to take
Start a public list for one area, built from groups that write in, and introduce them to each other. See "Add your organization" below.

Start a link-up in five steps

  1. Write down every small group you know in your area. Ten is plenty. Include the ones that are just a group chat.
  2. Contact each one. Use the email below, or call. Ask for one person's name and number, not a general inbox.
  3. Pick a time and a free room. A library meeting room, a church hall, a coffee shop, or a video call. First one in three weeks, not three months.
  4. Bring one sheet. Every group, what it does, who to call. Hand it out. That sheet is the network.
  5. Do it again next month. Add whoever shows up. Drop the parts nobody uses.

The first email

Subject: Small groups in [area], one hour a month?

Hi [name], I'm with [your group]. We keep meeting people who need the kind of help you offer, and we don't always know where to send them. Would you join a one-hour call or coffee with a few other small groups in [area]? No agenda beyond learning what each of us does and who to call. First one: [date, time, place]. Reply with a yes or a better date.

Add your organization
