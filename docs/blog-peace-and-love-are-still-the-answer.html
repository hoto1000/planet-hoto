Peace and Love Are Still the Answer

By Hoto · October 24, 2026 · What I believe

It sounds like a bumper sticker. I am going to argue it is closer to an engineering principle: the only strategy that does not need an endless supply of enemies to keep working. And then I want to ask what we do with that, once we admit it.

My point is that peace and love are not the soft option. They are the only options that scale. Every alternative, whether it is revenge, domination, or contempt, runs on a fuel that eventually runs out, which is other people's willingness to be hurt without hurting back. Peace and love are the only strategies that get stronger the more people adopt them.

Why this matters to me is that I run a site for people in hard times, and I have noticed something. Almost nobody arrives here because of a shortage of money or medicine, though those are real. They arrive because something broke between them and another person, or between them and themselves. The practical guides fix logistics. This post is about the thing underneath the logistics.

What "always" actually means

The objection arrives before I finish the sentence: what about Hitler? Fine. Let me say what I mean by "always" so we are not arguing with a straw man.

Peace is always the answer to the question "what are we trying to get to?" It is not always the answer to "what do I do in the next ten seconds?" A person being attacked may defend themselves. A country invaded may fight. But the moment the fighting is treated as the goal rather than the detour, you have lost the plot, and most wars lose the plot in the first week. The test for any hard action is simple: does it bring the day of peace nearer, or does it just feel like justice? Those are often different days.

Love is the same. I do not mean approval. I do not mean liking. I mean the decision to treat another person as a person, even while opposing what they do. Martin Luther King Jr. was clear about this in a sermon in 1957: you do not have to like your enemy, and you are not asked to. You are asked not to let hate become the thing that runs you. "Hate cannot drive out hate," he wrote a few years later, "only love can do that." He was not being sweet. He was describing a mechanism.

Here is what the evidence supports

  • Nonviolence wins more often. Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan studied over three hundred campaigns for regime change or self-determination between 1900 and 2006. Nonviolent campaigns succeeded about twice as often as violent ones, and the countries they won in were more likely to end up democratic and less likely to fall back into civil war. This is the single most important fact in this post and almost nobody knows it.
  • Contact dissolves contempt. A meta-analysis of more than five hundred studies found that real contact between groups who dislike each other reliably reduces prejudice, and the effect holds across countries, ages, and the kind of group. Hatred needs distance. It does badly at close range.
  • Love is the best predictor we have of a good life. The Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed the same families since 1938. Its headline finding, repeated by every director it has had, is that the quality of close relationships predicts health and happiness in old age better than wealth, fame, or cholesterol.
  • Former enemies can become boring neighbours. France and Germany fought three wars in seventy years and have fought none in the eighty since. Northern Ireland buried more than three thousand people in thirty years and then signed an agreement in 1998 that, for all its strain, has held. These were not miracles. They were negotiations, with compromises that made everyone a little sick, and they worked.

What is uncertain: whether humanity as a whole has become less violent over centuries, as Steven Pinker argues, is genuinely disputed by historians and statisticians, and I am not resting anything on it. The Chenoweth data ends in 2006, and the success rate of nonviolent campaigns has fallen since, partly because governments learned to counter them. The claim is not that peace always wins. It is that it wins more often than the alternative, at a fraction of the cost.

How we evolve from this fact

Evolution is the right word, and not in the inspirational-poster sense. Evolution is slow, it happens through many small selections, and it does not require anyone to be perfect. It requires that the better pattern gets copied a little more often than the worse one. So here is what copying it looks like, at three sizes.

In yourself. Make love the default and let the exceptions prove themselves. Most of us do it the other way round: suspicion first, warmth on evidence. Flip it. You will be wrong occasionally and you will lose less than you think, because the people who exploit kindness are rarer than the people who are starving for it. And learn forgiveness as a skill, not a feeling. It is a thing you do, repeatedly, usually before you feel like it.

In your street. Go near the people you have been told to despise. Not online, where contact makes things worse, but in a room, with food if possible. The research on contact is clear that it works best as equals doing something together, so join the thing: the school committee, the allotment, the five-a-side, the choir with the bad tenor section. It is almost impossible to keep hating a category once a specific member of it has lent you a chair.

In your politics. Judge every policy by one question: does it reduce the number of people who want to hurt us, or increase it? Bombing increases it. Humiliation increases it. Feeding people, educating people, and treating their grievances as real tends to reduce it. This is not naive. It is the cheapest security policy ever devised, and it is the one every victorious power eventually adopts toward the people it defeated, which rather suggests we could skip the defeating part.

And in how we talk. Stop sharing the thing that makes you hate. You know the one. The clip, the account, the group chat that exists to keep you angry. Every time you forward it, you are selecting for the worse pattern. Every time you don't, you are selecting for the better one. That is the whole mechanism of evolution, and you have your thumb on it.

Who could be affected, and what might I be missing

The strongest counterpoint is that peace without justice is just quiet. A ceasefire that leaves the boot on someone's neck is not peace, and telling the person under the boot to choose love is an insult. I accept that completely. The answer is that love includes the person under the boot, which means peace has to include their freedom, or it is not the thing I am describing. Peace is not the absence of conflict. It is conflict handled without cruelty.

The second counterpoint is that some actors cannot be reached, and that a pacifist beside a murderer is just a slower victim. True, and the answer is boundaries. Love does not mean handing someone the knife twice. You can refuse, resist, restrain, and still decline to hate. The prison guard who treats the prisoner as human has not gone soft. He has refused to let the prisoner's crime decide what kind of person he is.

And the people who pay for a naive peace are the weak, not the people preaching it. So I hold myself to this: if a policy I call peaceful leaves the vulnerable less safe, I was wrong, and I will say so.

One step I want to take

Mine is to find one person I have written off this year, and send one message that assumes they are a person. Not an apology, necessarily, and not a debate. Just a door. Yours could be the same, or it could be to leave one group chat that exists to keep you angry. Small is fine. Evolution is small. It just has to go in the right direction more often than not.

Hate is a loan at a terrible rate. Love is the only thing I have found that pays you back more than you put in, and it pays everyone around you too.

If you are here because something is broken between you and someone else, the relationships guide has the practical scripts. If it is broken between you and yourself, start with finding care. And if tonight is one of the bad ones, the strip at the top of the page is there for you.

Where the facts come from

Sources

  • Martin Luther King Jr., "Loving Your Enemies," sermon at Dexter Avenue Baptist Church, 1957, at the Stanford King Institute. The "hate cannot drive out hate" line is from his 1963 book Strength to Love.
  • Erica Chenoweth and Maria J. Stephan, Why Civil Resistance Works (Columbia University Press, 2011). Nonviolent campaigns from 1900 to 2006 succeeded roughly twice as often as violent ones. Chenoweth's later work notes the success rate has fallen since 2010.
  • Pettigrew & Tropp (2006), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. A meta-analysis of 515 studies on intergroup contact and prejudice.
  • The Harvard Study of Adult Development, running since 1938, on close relationships as the strongest predictor of late-life health and happiness.
  • The Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement, 1998, full text from the UK government.
  • Steven Pinker, The Better Angels of Our Nature (2011), for the long-decline-in-violence argument, which I mention only to flag that it is contested and that this post does not depend on it.
  • The earlier post on war on this blog, for the argument about who sells wars and who pays for them.

This post is my opinion. The facts in it are sourced above, and the uncertain parts are marked in the text.
